Religious and Philosophical Traditions of South and East Asia

Hinduism

Hinduism is generally regarded as the oldest continuously practised major religious tradition, with roots in the Vedic religion brought to the Indian subcontinent by Indo-Aryan migrants from around 1500 BCE. The earliest texts, the Vedas, are collections of hymns, rituals, and philosophical speculation composed in Sanskrit over many centuries. Later texts known as the Upanishads, composed from around 800 BCE onward, shifted the tradition's focus toward philosophical questions about the nature of the self (atman) and its relationship to the ultimate reality underlying the universe (Brahman).

Central concepts in Hindu thought include karma, the principle that intentional actions have consequences that shape a person's future circumstances, including future lives, and dharma, a person's duty or right conduct, which varies according to their stage of life and social position. Samsara refers to the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth that karma perpetuates, while moksha refers to liberation from this cycle, considered the ultimate spiritual goal in most Hindu schools of thought. The Bhagavad Gita, part of the much larger epic the Mahabharata and composed roughly in the last centuries BCE, is one of the most widely read Hindu texts; framed as a dialogue between the warrior Arjuna and the god Krishna, it addresses the tension between duty and non-violence and introduces the idea of selfless action performed without attachment to its results.

Unlike the Abrahamic traditions, Hinduism does not trace itself to a single founder or a single canonical text, and it encompasses a wide diversity of practice, from devotion to a personal deity such as Vishnu or Shiva, to philosophical schools that treat the divine as an impersonal, all-pervading reality.

Buddhism

Buddhism was founded by Siddhartha Gautama, a prince from the Shakya clan in present-day Nepal, traditionally dated to the sixth or fifth century BCE. According to tradition, after witnessing suffering in the forms of old age, sickness, and death, Gautama renounced his privileged life to seek the cause of suffering and the means of ending it, eventually achieving enlightenment and becoming known as the Buddha, or "awakened one."

The Buddha's core teaching is summarised in the Four Noble Truths: that life inherently involves suffering (dukkha); that suffering arises from craving and attachment; that the cessation of suffering is possible; and that the path to this cessation is the Eightfold Path, comprising right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. Unlike Hindu thought at the time, early Buddhism rejected the authority of the Vedas and the caste-based structure of Vedic society, and taught that liberation was available to anyone who followed the path, regardless of social status.

Buddhism spread well beyond its place of origin, particularly after the Mauryan emperor Ashoka converted in the third century BCE following a violent military campaign and subsequently sponsored the spread of Buddhist teaching and missionary activity across South and Southeast Asia. Over the following centuries, Buddhism divided into distinct branches, most notably Theravada Buddhism, which emphasises the monastic path and adheres closely to the earliest recorded teachings, and Mahayana Buddhism, which developed a broader range of texts, emphasises the ideal of the bodhisattva who delays their own final liberation to help others, and became dominant in China, Korea, Japan, and Tibet.

Confucianism and Daoism in China

Confucianism and Daoism both emerged in China during the Warring States period, a time of political fragmentation and conflict in the centuries before unification under the Qin dynasty in 221 BCE, and both responded to the same underlying question of how to restore social and political order, though they proposed very different answers.

Confucius (traditionally 551 to 479 BCE) taught that social harmony depended on individuals fulfilling clearly defined roles and obligations within a hierarchy of relationships, such as ruler and subject, parent and child, and older and younger sibling, guided by virtues including ren (benevolence), li (ritual propriety), and filial piety, or respect for one's parents and ancestors. Confucianism is often described less as a religion in the conventional sense and more as an ethical and political philosophy, though it developed rituals, including ancestor veneration, and was later treated as a state orthodoxy under several Chinese dynasties.

Daoism, traditionally associated with the semi-legendary figure Laozi and the text attributed to him, the Dao De Jing, took a markedly different approach, emphasising alignment with the Dao, or "the Way," an underlying natural order that cannot be fully captured in words or rigid social rules. Where Confucianism stressed active cultivation of virtue and correct ritual behaviour to maintain order, Daoism promoted wu wei, or effortless action in harmony with nature, and was generally sceptical of imposed hierarchy and elaborate ceremony as a route to social harmony.

Shinto in Japan

Shinto is the indigenous religious tradition of Japan, centred on the veneration of kami, spirits or divine forces associated with natural features such as mountains, rivers, and trees, as well as with ancestors and legendary figures. Unlike the Abrahamic traditions or Buddhism, Shinto has no single founder, no central canonical scripture comparable to the Quran or the Vedas, and no strict, codified system of doctrine; instead, it is expressed primarily through ritual practice at shrines, seasonal festivals, and purification rites. Shinto has coexisted with Buddhism in Japan since the latter's introduction in the sixth century CE, and for much of Japanese history the two traditions were closely intertwined, with many shrines and temples historically sharing sites and practices, a blending generally referred to as shinbutsu-shugo.
