The Abrahamic Traditions: Judaism, Christianity and Islam

Judaism

Judaism traces its origins to the patriarch Abraham, traditionally dated to around the early second millennium BCE, who according to the Hebrew Bible entered into a covenant with a single God. The formative national narrative of ancient Israelite religion is the Exodus, the story of the Israelites' escape from slavery in Egypt under the leadership of Moses, followed by the receiving of the Law, or Torah, at Mount Sinai. The Torah, comprising the first five books of the Hebrew Bible, contains both narrative material and a detailed legal and ethical code that continues to shape Jewish religious life.

The First Temple in Jerusalem, built under King Solomon around the 10th century BCE, served as the central site of Israelite worship and sacrifice until its destruction by the Babylonians in 586 BCE, an event followed by the Babylonian Exile. The Second Temple, completed around 516 BCE after the return from exile, remained the centre of Jewish religious life until its destruction by Rome in 70 CE during the Jewish-Roman War. Following this destruction, Judaism reorganised around synagogue worship, prayer, and rabbinic study of the Torah and Talmud rather than temple sacrifice, a transformation often referred to as the shift to Rabbinic Judaism. The subsequent centuries saw a widespread Jewish diaspora across the Mediterranean world, the Middle East, and eventually Europe.

Christianity

Christianity emerged from within first-century Judaism as a movement centred on Jesus of Nazareth, a Jewish preacher and teacher active in Roman-occupied Judea. His followers proclaimed him the Messiah and, after his crucifixion under the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate around 30 to 33 CE, believed he had risen from the dead. The apostle Paul was particularly influential in spreading the new faith beyond Jewish communities to Gentile audiences throughout the Roman Empire, arguing that adherence to Jewish ritual law was not required for followers of Christ.

For its first three centuries, Christianity was a minority and at times persecuted religion within the Roman Empire. This changed decisively with the Edict of Milan in 313 CE, issued by the emperor Constantine, which granted religious tolerance to Christians, and with Christianity's eventual establishment as the state religion of the Empire under Theodosius I in 380 CE. As the church grew, so did disputes over doctrine, particularly concerning the nature of Christ. The Council of Nicaea, convened by Constantine in 325 CE, was the first major attempt to resolve this dispute at an empire-wide level. It produced the Nicene Creed, which affirmed that Christ was "of one substance" with God the Father, rejecting the rival position known as Arianism, which held that Christ was a created being subordinate to God.

Doctrinal and political tensions between the Latin-speaking western church, centred on Rome, and the Greek-speaking eastern church, centred on Constantinople, grew over subsequent centuries over issues including papal authority and liturgical practice. These tensions culminated in the East-West Schism of 1054, in which the Pope and the Patriarch of Constantinople excommunicated one another, formally dividing Christianity into what became the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church, a division that has never been fully healed.

Islam

Islam originated in the early seventh century CE in the Arabian Peninsula through the teachings of the prophet Muhammad, who Muslims believe received revelations from God, compiled after his death into the Quran, Islam's central religious text. Muhammad's message, delivered first in Mecca and later in Medina after his migration (the Hijra) in 622 CE, emphasised strict monotheism, submission to God's will, and social and economic justice.

Central to Islamic practice are the Five Pillars: the shahada (declaration of faith), salat (five daily prayers), zakat (almsgiving to the poor), sawm (fasting during the month of Ramadan), and hajj (pilgrimage to Mecca at least once for those who are able). Following Muhammad's death in 632 CE, a dispute arose over rightful succession to lead the Muslim community. Those who believed leadership should pass to Muhammad's father-in-law, Abu Bakr, and subsequent elected caliphs became known as Sunni Muslims, while those who believed leadership should have passed to Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, Ali, and his descendants became known as Shia Muslims. This succession dispute, rather than a primarily theological disagreement, is generally identified as the root of the Sunni-Shia split that continues to shape the Muslim world today.
