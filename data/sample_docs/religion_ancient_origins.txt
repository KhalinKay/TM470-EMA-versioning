Origins and Early Development of Religion

Prehistoric Religious Practice

The earliest evidence for religious behaviour comes from burial sites rather than written texts. Neanderthal and early Homo sapiens graves dating back tens of thousands of years contain ochre pigment, tools, and animal bones placed deliberately alongside the dead, suggesting a belief in some form of continued existence after death. Cave art from sites such as Lascaux and Chauvet in France, created between roughly 30,000 and 17,000 years ago, depicts animals and occasionally half-human, half-animal figures, which many scholars interpret as evidence of shamanic practice: a belief that certain individuals could mediate between the human world and a spirit world.

Two broad categories are commonly used to describe prehistoric belief systems. Animism is the belief that natural objects, places, and phenomena possess a spirit or life force; it is associated with hunter-gatherer societies who depended directly on their environment. Ancestor worship, in which deceased family members are venerated and believed to influence the living, appears in burial customs across many early agricultural societies and remains a feature of several living religious traditions today.

Mesopotamian Polytheism

With the rise of settled agriculture and the first cities in Mesopotamia (modern-day Iraq) around 3500 BCE, religion became more organised and closely tied to the state. The Sumerians, and later the Akkadians and Babylonians, worshipped a large pantheon of gods associated with natural forces and city-states: Anu (sky), Enlil (storm and kingship), Enki (water and wisdom), and Inanna/Ishtar (love and war) were among the most prominent. Temples, known as ziggurats, functioned simultaneously as religious centres and as administrative and economic hubs, with priests overseeing grain storage and taxation as well as ritual.

The Babylonian creation myth, the Enuma Elish, composed around the 18th to 16th century BCE, describes the god Marduk defeating the chaotic sea goddess Tiamat and creating the world from her body. The text was recited annually during the Babylonian New Year festival and served a political purpose: it legitimised Marduk's supremacy among the gods and, by extension, Babylon's supremacy among cities. This close relationship between cosmology and political authority is a recurring pattern in ancient Near Eastern religion.

Ancient Egyptian Religion

Egyptian religion, which developed continuously from around 3100 BCE until the spread of Christianity in the fourth century CE, centred on the concept of Ma'at: a principle of cosmic order, balance, and justice that both gods and the pharaoh were responsible for upholding. The Egyptian pantheon included Ra (the sun god), Osiris (god of the afterlife and resurrection), Isis, and Horus, among many local and regional deities that were absorbed into the wider pantheon over time.

Egyptian religion placed extraordinary emphasis on the afterlife. The practice of mummification, the construction of elaborate tombs and pyramids, and the Book of the Dead (a collection of spells intended to guide the deceased through the underworld) all reflect a belief that a person's soul could achieve a form of eternal life, provided the correct rituals were performed and the heart was judged favourably against the feather of Ma'at.

A notable, though short-lived, exception to Egyptian polytheism occurred under the pharaoh Akhenaten (reigned approximately 1353 to 1336 BCE), who is closely associated with a religious reform that elevated Aten, the sun disc, above all other gods and suppressed the worship of Amun and the traditional priesthood. Some historians describe this as an early experiment in monotheism, though scholars disagree about how absolute the exclusivity of Aten worship really was. After Akhenaten's death, his successors, including Tutankhamun, restored the traditional pantheon and reversed most of his religious changes.

Greek and Roman Religion

Ancient Greek religion was polytheistic and closely woven into civic life, with each city-state typically adopting a patron deity, such as Athena for Athens. The Olympian gods, including Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, and Apollo, were understood not as all-powerful or morally perfect but as powerful beings with human-like flaws who could be appeased through sacrifice, festivals, and oracles. The Oracle of Delphi, where a priestess called the Pythia delivered prophecies believed to originate from Apollo, was consulted by individuals and city-states alike on matters ranging from personal decisions to warfare.

Alongside civic religion, mystery cults such as the Eleusinian Mysteries offered initiates secret rites and the promise of a better fate after death, in contrast to the vaguer afterlife described in mainstream Greek belief. Roman religion adopted much of the Greek pantheon under different names (Jupiter for Zeus, Venus for Aphrodite) but placed greater emphasis on precise ritual performance and state cult, including the eventual worship of deified emperors, as a means of maintaining the favour of the gods toward Rome itself.
